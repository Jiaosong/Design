## Research Question
How can Papanek's critique of industrial design, consumerism, exclusion, and environmental responsibility be translated into executable design decisions without copying a low-tech / DIY / eco visual style?

## Source-grounded facts
- Victor J. Papanek (1923–1998) was a designer, educator, and author. The Victor J. Papanek Foundation at the University of Applied Arts Vienna holds his archive and library.
- Key publications include *Design for the Real World* (English edition 1971), *Nomadic Furniture I* (1973), *Nomadic Furniture II* (1974), *How Things Don't Work* (1977), *Design for Human Scale* (1983), and *The Green Imperative* (1995).
- Vitra Design Museum's 2018–2019 retrospective positioned his work within social, ecological, and political design debates and drew on the Papanek estate.
- Alison J. Clarke's archival history also documents contradictions in social design, including Cold War development intervention, quasi-anthropological approaches, and neocolonial power relations.

## OLEANDER inference
Affected actors → harm/exclusion risk → real-use context → resource/material consequence → accessible/repairable alternatives → trade-off → evidence & feedback.

This is an OLEANDER analytical model, not a quotation or original Papanek formula.

## Transferable rules
1. Put affected actors and possible harm/exclusion before styling.
2. Treat real-use capability, resource constraints, maintenance, geography, and institutional context as design inputs.
3. Do not turn low-resource conditions into a visual aesthetic shortcut.
4. Bring material, manufacturing, use, maintenance, replacement, and disposal consequences into comparison; no environmental-performance claims without evidence.
5. Social responsibility does not authorize the designer to decide for others; participation, research ethics, and power relations remain separate evidence requirements.

## Non-transferable / critical boundary
Papanek must not be flattened into anti-business, anti-industry, DIY, or eco-style. Historical “design for others” practices require contemporary scrutiny because benevolent intent does not eliminate unequal power relations.
